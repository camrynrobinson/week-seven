- understanding what a network represents (people, objects, events, places...) and keeping it in mind while you work is, as obvious as it sounds, fundamental to keeping your work focused and effective.
- SG (paraphrasing): If we start discussing assemblages of objects like people, we slip back into the old school of archaeology wherein we are also treating people like objects.
- When we interact with an artifact or a person or a ruin, we are interacting with a single point in time and space which, in combination with other individual points at a similar time and/or space, produce a history of that moment. public history!
- SG (paraphrasing): we do not find evidence of the Roman Empire, we find evidence of individual experiences in a given area and a given time period, which we put together to visualize the Roman Empire.
- these can be difficult to quantify; archaeology requires complex (not complicated) tools and systems, which can flex and adapt to changing parameters and resources.
    - Knowledge graphs: multidimensional networks relating various types and weights of relationship; produces numerical values for complex relationships: (from lecture slide) "different 'kinds' of facts become easier to find than others. every fact represents a different direction through that space; you can measure distance and angle between facts to infer new information"
- Romanowska et al. (2019): "The core idea behind complexity science is the observation that
simple interactions of multiple entities may lead to surprising global patterns and that this connection could not be easily deduced from studying each of the system’s elements in isolation"(2).
- small-world connections have been argued to be the core, necessary element which gives rise to self-organization. this is how feedback enters the network: small world connections are the source of self-organizing criticality, providing a mechanism through which we can understand how a network (ie. society) operates (six degrees of Kevin Bacon).

- Brughmans(2010): "in complex archaeological datasets a wide variety of relationships can be identified. Therefore, the need was expressed to define every component of a network from the outset, as any conclusions drawn from an analysis of networks should be completely interpreted in light of these definitions"(298).
